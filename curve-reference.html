Section 01 · Foundations
1

What is a curve

A curve maps the market's expectation of a price at every future maturity — constructed so it exactly reprices every liquid instrument used to build it. For a European power and gas utility, the price forward curve is the methodological backbone of generation fleet valuation, supply portfolio marking, hedge sizing, and regulatory reporting.

Job 01 — Price Forward Curve (PFC)
Expected delivery price
The PFC answers: what price does the market expect for power or gas delivery at each future date? For power it is the expected €/MWh for base or peak delivery in the German DE-LU/AT zone, UK N2EX, or Dutch APX market. For gas it is the €/MWh at TTF or NCG. This is the central input to every generation dispatch model, supply contract valuation, and hedge position at E.ON.
Job 02 — Non-storability: the defining constraint
Power has no cost-of-carry
For storable commodities (gas, metals), the forward price is anchored by storage economics: F(T) = S₀ · e^((r+s−y)T). For power, this relationship does not exist — electricity cannot be stored across delivery periods. The forward price is a pure market expectation of supply/demand balance at that future date, anchored at the long end by fuel-cost economics: gas × heat rate + carbon × EF + O&M. With no physical carry to discipline the curve, governance does all the work.
Core principle — power non-storability

A power forward curve can imply calendar spreads that would be instantly arbitraged away in any storable market — and they persist because no one can carry physical electricity from January to February. This absence of physical discipline is why curve governance — parameter approval cycles, methodology notes, sign-off on overlays, validation logs — is not a compliance overhead for power: it is the only mechanism that enforces consistency. Every other commodity has physics as a backstop. Power does not.

Section 02 · Foundations
2

Forward curve shape

The power forward curve is the strip of market-implied €/MWh prices for base and peak delivery at each future period, extracted from traded exchange and OTC instruments. Its shape carries information about seasonal demand, fuel economics, capacity adequacy, and renewable penetration that simple interpolation cannot replicate.

Shape — Contango (deferred > nearby)
Market expects price rise
In power markets, contango across seasons typically reflects expected demand growth, tighter supply conditions ahead, or a structural winter premium building as the heating season approaches. Unlike in gas, contango cannot be locked in by a storage-and-forward trade — it persists only as long as the market consensus holds it.
Shape — Backwardation (nearby > deferred)
Prompt tightness or scarcity
Near-term prices above forward expectations signals immediate supply/demand tightness — a generator outage, cold snap, low wind output, or gas supply disruption. Power spot markets (EPEX) spike on scarcity because no storage buffer absorbs the imbalance. A curve in steep near-term backwardation signals a stressed system.
E.ON markets — Multi-scale seasonal structure
The German/Continental power forward curve has a pronounced annual seasonal structure: winter Cal (Q4+Q1) trades at a significant premium to summer Cal (Q2+Q3) driven by heating demand and lower renewable generation. Within quarters, base and peak quotes diverge by 15–50% depending on season. Within days — observable in EPEX day-ahead settlements — the morning ramp, noon solar suppression, and evening demand peak can span 50+ €/MWh even outside stress conditions. The forward curve must preserve this multi-scale seasonal structure — not smooth it. A flat interpolation between quarterly anchors is commercially wrong.
Section 03 · Foundations
3

Why we need them

For a vertically integrated European energy utility, the price forward curve flows through every business function that touches price exposure. Its accuracy and governance directly affect P&L reporting, risk limits, hedging effectiveness, and regulatory capital requirements.

  • Generation Mark-to-market of the generation fleet — forward power sales, gas and carbon purchase hedges, PPAs, CfDs, and tolling agreements all require a consistent forward curve. Without it, valuations across business units diverge and P&L aggregation is meaningless.
  • Supply Retail and B2B supply portfolios with fixed-price and indexed contracts require a PFC to mark the procurement book, size the hedging requirement by delivery period, and compute margin. A flat or stale curve produces phantom hedging surpluses or deficits that are invisible until settlement.
  • Risk PFE on open supply positions, VaR and CVaR on the generation margin book, clean spark spread sensitivity (DV01 per €/MWh of power, gas, and carbon), and scenario P&L all require the curve as a common input. Inconsistent curves across desks produce a risk figure that cannot be aggregated.
  • Margin / Collateral EEX and ICE clearing margin calls, bilateral CSA variation margin, and collateral optimization across power, gas, and carbon hedges are all resolved against the reference forward price. Disputes with counterparties over variation margin are fundamentally disputes about the curve. Governance determines whether those disputes are resolved in hours or become operational risk events.
Definition — No-arbitrage in power markets

Arbitrage is a risk-free profit from a price inconsistency. For power, the no-arbitrage condition is purely financial — there is no physical mechanism to trade it away. A power forward curve that implies a Q1/Q2 spread tradeable for risk-free profit against exchange-quoted instruments is a wrong curve. The curve must reprice every calibrating instrument it was built from, or a financial arbitrage exists against it. Unlike in gas or metals, no warehousing trade will automatically correct it.

Section 04 · Methods
4

Curve models

No single construction method is correct for all maturities. The choice is a governance decision: each method occupies a different position on the flexibility / transparency / stability triangle. In power and gas markets, the maturity zones map naturally to the liquidity structure of traded instruments.

Method 01
Sequential Bootstrapping
Build the curve node-by-node from the short end. Each calibrating instrument is solved to exactly zero repricing error before moving to the next maturity. Every pillar is independently auditable — the price at each node is the direct market quote, unmodified by any smoothing.
Use when: near-curve (0–3M power from EEX day-ahead and ICE month-ahead; 0–3M gas from TTF/NCG prompt-month settlement). Instruments are liquid with tight bid-ask; exact reprice is required for settlement and audit.
Method 02
Best-fit / Solver
Solve all curve points jointly via a nonlinear least-squares solver minimising weighted pricing error. Handles overlapping instruments — quarterly swaps that span multiple monthly pillars — cleanly. Repricing errors are minimised but not guaranteed zero.
Use when: mid-curve with overlapping OTC instruments (power quarterly/annual swaps; TTF seasonal storage swaps). Robustness to overlap matters more than exact reprice.
Method 03
Spline Interpolation
Fit a smooth piecewise-polynomial (cubic or tension spline) through calibrated pillar prices. Guarantees continuous first and second derivatives. Can produce oscillation between sparse pillars — especially in seasonal markets where adjacent quarterly prices differ substantially.
Use when: seasonal mid-curve (3M–2Y) for power and gas where smooth shape is required for risk aggregation. Use natural or clamped boundary conditions. Never accept negative implied forward prices from an unconstrained spline.
Method 04
Seasonal Shaping
Decompose base/peak calendar or quarterly quotes into hourly or daily granularity via a load or temperature shape model. Converts the quarterly anchor the market quotes into the intra-period delivery profile that EPEX settles. Must be versioned, formally approved, and reviewed when the generation mix changes.
Use when: hourly or daily granularity is required — EPEX settlement, structured product valuation (base+peak decomposition), CCGT dispatch optimization. Not optional: it is the bridge between what the market quotes and what physically settles.
Method 05
Factor / PCA Model
Reduce the curve to orthogonal factors (level, slope, curvature) estimated from historical covariance of the futures strip. Stable and parsimonious; well-behaved extrapolation to illiquid tenors. Cannot reprice every pillar exactly; misspecification risk if seasonal structure is strong.
Use when: sparse illiquid long end (2Y+ for power and gas), scenario generation, stress testing, or VaR computation where curve-factor decomposition is needed. A seasonal factor (4th PCA component in energy) should be explicitly identified.
Section 05 · Methods

How we price power & gas positions

The present value of any commodity position is the sum of discounted forward cashflows over every delivery period. For power and gas, the complexity is in what you put into F(tᵢ) and Qᵢ — not in the formula itself.

# Present value of a power or gas forward position

PV  =  Σ  P(tᵢ)  ×  F(tᵢ)  ×  Qᵢ     summed over delivery periods i = 1 … N

  P(tᵢ)  =  discount factor at delivery time tᵢ   =  1 / (1 + r(tᵢ))^tᵢ
  F(tᵢ)  =  forward price for delivery at tᵢ      (from the price forward curve, €/MWh)
  Qᵢ     =  quantity delivered in period i         (MWh for power; MWh or therm for gas)

Six factors determine whether this formula produces a number that is commercially correct or technically precise but commercially wrong:

  1. 1
    Delivery profile
    Qᵢ is not a single number — it is a per-hour or per-day quantity reflecting the actual delivery schedule: base vs peak hours, weekday vs weekend, swing volume rights. Getting the profile wrong mis-sizes every hedge. A quarterly base sale against a peak delivery contract is an unhedged peak exposure disguised as a flat position.
  2. 2
    Non-storability (power) vs storage economics (gas)
    For power, F(tᵢ) is a pure supply/demand expectation — no carry bound constrains it. For gas, the forward is bounded by storage economics (winter–summer spread ≥ inject + withdraw + working gas financing). When building a mixed power/gas position P&L, the different anchoring mechanisms must be reflected in how curve errors propagate.
  3. 3
    Seasonality
    F(tᵢ) varies structurally across the year — by month, week, and hour. Using a quarterly average for an intra-quarter delivery ignores seasonal peaks. In German power, January and February prices can be 15–30% above Q1 base. Peak hours can trade 25–50% above base in winter. This is not noise — it is structure that must be preserved.
  4. 4
    Cross-commodity coupling (generation margin)
    Power F(tᵢ) is not independent of gas and carbon prices. The clean spark spread — F_power − F_gas × HR − F_carbon × EF — is the CCGT generation margin at each delivery period. A position priced solely on the power curve without accounting for gas and carbon inputs is a position with three open commodity legs, not one.
  5. 5
    Optionality
    Tolling agreements, swing gas contracts, virtual PPAs, indexed supply contracts, and embedded generation optionality cannot be priced by the forward curve alone — they require a volatility surface or stochastic model. The PV formula above is the deterministic core; add an option-adjusted model on top for optionality.
  6. 6
    Granularity gap
    EEX quotes quarterly base and peak — EPEX settles hourly. The shape model fills this gap. Any error in the shape model flows directly into F(tᵢ) and is indistinguishable from a market price movement in P&L attribution. An ungoverned shape model is a hidden P&L source.
Recurring trap — granularity mismatch
The most common pricing error in power desks is using a quarterly base forward price as a proxy for an intra-quarter peak delivery. A Q1 German base price applied to January peak hours over-prices February base while under-pricing January peak — producing a P&L that looks flat in aggregate but oscillates sharply within the quarter. Always decompose to settlement granularity before discounting.

Expected future price — formulas & methods

The expected future commodity price at maturity T is the market's best estimate of where the delivery price will be at that date. For power it is a pure supply-demand expectation anchored to fuel-cost economics. For gas it is disciplined by storage carry. The formulas show how to calculate it and how the layers — carry, mean-reversion, seasonality — combine.

Gas (storable) — carry-anchored
The expected gas price is anchored by storage economics: F(T) = S₀ · e^((r+s−y)·T). Physical storage enforces this — if the winter/summer spread falls below inject + withdraw costs, operators arbitrage the spread by injection. E.ON's gas storage assets (Rehden, Haidach) provide internal reference for calibrating the storage cost parameters.
Power (non-storable) — governance-anchored
No cost-of-carry exists. The expected price is a market consensus on future supply/demand balance, anchored at the long end by fuel-cost parity: F ≈ F_gas × HR + F_carbon × EF + O&M. No physical mechanism enforces it. Governance does.
Eq 01 Cost-of-carry forward price (gas / storable commodities)

For gas, the no-arbitrage forward price is determined by the cost of buying spot and holding to delivery. Winter/summer spread violations create physical storage arbitrages traded away in liquid markets.

F(0, T)  =  S₀  ·  e^((r + s − y) · T)

  S₀  =  spot price today  (€/MWh for TTF)
  r   =  EUR risk-free rate  (€STR, continuously compounded)
  s   =  storage cost rate  (per year: injection + withdrawal + working gas financing)
  y   =  convenience yield  (per year: benefit from holding physical gas inventory)
  T   =  time to delivery in years

# Storage no-arbitrage bound (gas governance hard constraint):
  Winter/summer spread  ≥  inject_cost + withdraw_cost + financing_cost
  Violation = documented storage arbitrage = hard governance flag, curve cannot publish.
Contango (r + s > y)
Storage costs exceed convenience yield. Gas summer prices below winter — deferred prices include the cost of storing summer gas for winter delivery. Standard injection-season structure.
Backwardation (y > r + s)
Scarcity premium exceeds carry. Nearby gas above forward — physical holders earn a premium. Typical in cold snaps or supply disruptions when buyers compete for immediate delivery.
Eq 02 Non-storable commodity — fuel-cost parity anchor (power)

For power, storage is impossible. The cost-of-carry formula does not apply. At the long end, the market-clearing price is anchored to the economics of the marginal generator — a CCGT in European markets.

# Power: no cost-of-carry relationship exists.
# Long-end anchor — fuel-cost parity of the marginal CCGT:

F_power(T)  ≈  F_gas(T) × HR  +  F_carbon(T) × EF  +  OM  +  capacity_premium(T)

  F_gas(T)   =  TTF/NCG forward price at T   (€/MWh)
  HR         =  heat rate of marginal CCGT   (~1.92–2.04 MWh_gas per MWh_el, = 49–52% eff.)
  F_carbon(T) =  EUA forward at T            (€/tCO₂)
  EF         =  CCGT emission factor         (~0.35–0.40 tCO₂/MWh_el)
  OM         =  variable O&M of marginal CCGT (~3–6 €/MWh)
  capacity_premium = scarcity premium above fuel cost; zero in normal conditions

# Use as long-end governance anchor / sanity check, not as primary construction input.
# Primary input is market-quoted EEX/ICE instruments for liquid near and mid curve.
Eq 03 Mean-reverting expected price — Ornstein-Uhlenbeck

Mean reversion is the defining dynamic of power and gas prices. Prices spike on scarcity then revert to seasonal equilibrium as supply responds. The OU process captures this dynamic — its expected value formula is the mathematical expression of that reversion.

# Stochastic differential equation:
dX  =  κ · (θ(t) − X) · dt  +  σ · dW

  κ     =  mean-reversion speed (yr⁻¹)  power: 3–10  gas: 1–5
  θ(t)  =  seasonal equilibrium at time t  (see Eq 05 for Fourier form)
  σ     =  volatility of the spot process
  dW    =  standard Brownian increment

# Closed-form expected value at horizon T:
E[X(T)]  =  θ  +  (X₀ − θ) · e^(−κ · T)

# As T → ∞,  E[X(T)] → θ  (long-run equilibrium)
# Half-life of a shock: T½ = ln(2) / κ  →  power: 1–3 months; gas: 2–8 months

# Calibrate κ from deseasonalised spot AR(1) on weekly EPEX/TTF settlements:
#   AR(1) coefficient φ = e^(−κΔt)  →  κ = −ln(φ)/Δt

Governance note: κ and σ must be re-estimated when the generation mix changes materially — large renewable capacity additions change the frequency and severity of spike events, making parameters calibrated in prior regimes unreliable for tail risk.

Eq 04 Two-factor expected price — Schwartz-Smith decomposition

The two-factor model separates spot dynamics into a fast-reverting short-term component (spikes, supply disruptions) and a slow-moving long-term equilibrium (fuel-cost anchored). This explains why power front-month and Cal+2 prices are nearly uncorrelated — driven by different factors.

# Decompose log spot into two latent factors:
ln S(t)  =  χ(t)  +  ξ(t)

  χ(t)  =  short-term deviation  →  fast mean-reversion  (κ_χ: power 3–10 yr⁻¹)
  ξ(t)  =  long-term equilibrium →  slow drift anchored to fuel-cost parity

# Factor dynamics:
dχ  =  −κ_χ · χ · dt  +  σ_χ · dW₁
dξ  =  μ_ξ · dt    +  σ_ξ · dW₂      Corr(dW₁, dW₂) = ρ

# Expected price at horizon T:
E[ln S(T)]  =  χ₀ · e^(−κ_χT) + ξ₀ + μ_ξT

# Calibrate {κ_χ, σ_χ, σ_ξ, μ_ξ, ρ} via Kalman filter on EEX/ICE futures strip.
# Identifying feature: term structure of futures volatility decreasing with maturity
#   → that term structure slope is the short-term factor's signature.

Practical use: the term structure of futures volatility (decreasing with maturity for power and gas) is the key identifying feature of the short-term factor. Use the Kalman filter on the historical forward strip to calibrate jointly.

Eq 05 Seasonal expected price — Fourier equilibrium

The long-run equilibrium θ is not a constant for energy — it is a deterministic seasonal function of time. Separating the deterministic seasonal component from the stochastic residual is the essential first step before calibrating any mean-reversion or volatility parameter.

# Additive seasonal mean-reversion:
E[S(T)]  =  θ(T)  +  (S₀ − θ(0)) · e^(−κ · T)

# Fourier seasonal equilibrium:
θ(T)  =  α₀  +  Σₖ  [aₖ · cos(2πkT/P)  +  bₖ · sin(2πkT/P)]

  α₀     =  long-run mean price level  (fuel-cost parity anchor for power)
  aₖ,bₖ =  Fourier coefficients for harmonic k  (estimated by OLS on historical data)
  P      =  period: 1 year for annual cycle, 1/52 for weekly
  k      =  harmonic; k=1 annual, k=2 semi-annual, k=3 quarterly …
  German power: 3–6 harmonics standard; fewer than 3 fails to capture sub-annual peaks

Governance note: the Fourier coefficients {aₖ, bₖ} are locked parameters — documented with number of harmonics, estimation window (minimum 5 years), and temperature-normalisation method applied. A change to any coefficient is a methodology change requiring formal sign-off.

Eq 06 Commodity hedging pressure & forward price bias

In power markets, generators (including E.ON generation) systematically sell forward to lock in generation margin, creating excess supply of forward contracts. Buyers of that forward risk earn a premium — the forward price tends to trade below the expected delivery price (Keynes' normal backwardation).

# Commodity forward price premium (Keynes normal backwardation):
F(0,T)  =  E[S(T)]  −  π(T)

  E[S(T)]  =  market's expected spot at delivery (fundamental value)
  π(T)     =  hedging premium paid by producers to buyers of forward price risk

# Power: generators are net forward sellers  →  π(T) > 0  →  forward < expected spot
# Gas:   producers are net short hedgers     →  similar sign to power (short tenors)
# EUAs:  compliance buyers create demand     →  forward ≥ expected spot

# Implication:
#   Valuation / M2M:          use the observed forward price  (contractual fair value)
#   Scenario / fundamental:   may use E[S(T)]  (add back estimated hedging premium)
#   Always state explicitly which you are computing.
Curve construction implication

For valuation and mark-to-market, always use the observed forward price from the market — it is the fair contractual value regardless of hedging premium. For scenario generation and fundamental price forecasting, you may want the expected spot price. The two are different numbers for different purposes — always be explicit about which one you are computing.

Eq 08 The complete forward price — all adjustments combined

The full expression for a power or gas forward curve makes each governance layer explicit — mean-reversion, seasonality, carry (gas only), and intra-period shape.

# Gas (storable):
F_gas(T)  =   [ θ_gas  +  (S₀ − θ_gas) · e^(−κT) ]   ← mean reversion to seasonal equilibrium
              ×  seasonal(T)                             ← Fourier seasonal adjustment
              ×  e^((r + s − y)T)                        ← cost of carry (storage economics)
              ×  shape(T)                                  ← intra-period delivery profile

# Power (non-storable):
F_power(T)  =  [ θ(T)  +  (S₀ − θ(0)) · e^(−κT) ]    ← mean reversion + seasonal equilibrium
               ×  shape(T)                                 ← base/peak intra-period profile
# No cost-of-carry term. Long-end θ(T) anchored to fuel-cost parity.

# Governance parameters:
  θ, κ         →  mean-reversion methodology note  (review: structural market or fuel change)
  seasonal(T)  →  seasonal model note               (review: generation mix change)
  r, s, y      →  gas inputs register               (review: storage tariff, rate change)
  shape(T)     →  shaping model approval record     (review: load profile / solar growth)
Method Step-by-step: building the power or gas PFC

An ordered procedure for E.ON's core energy products.

  1. Bootstrap the observable near end. Take exchange-quoted EEX (power) or ICE/EEX TTF (gas) settlement prices for 0–3 months. Assign F(tᵢ) = market quote directly. Exact reprice — no model, no assumption.
  2. Estimate the long-run equilibrium θ. For gas: long-run marginal cost of supply (LNG netback, production cost). For power: fuel-cost parity — F_gas × heat rate + F_carbon × EF + O&M of the marginal CCGT. Document the CCGT efficiency assumption and review frequency.
  3. Calibrate mean-reversion speed κ. Regress deseasonalised spot changes on lagged level: ΔX_t = −κ·X_{t-1}·Δt + ε. Report half-life T½ = ln(2)/κ alongside κ. Review when renewable generation capacity changes materially in the zone.
  4. Extract and lock seasonal parameters. Regress historical spot on Fourier basis features. Lock the coefficients as governance parameters. Document estimation window (minimum 5 years for annual cycle) and temperature normalisation applied.
  5. Compute E[S(T)] = θ(T) + (S₀ − θ(0)) · e^(−κT) at every required delivery horizon.
  6. Apply cost-of-carry adjustment (gas only). Multiply by e^((r+s−y)T) using EUR €STR rate, documented TTF storage injection/withdrawal cost, and calibrated convenience yield. Verify winter/summer spread satisfies the storage-bound constraint.
  7. Decompose to delivery granularity. Apply shape model to convert base/quarterly pillars into intra-period hourly prices. Record the shape model version number in the audit log. For power: base/peak; for gas: within-day profile for swing valuation.
  8. Validate. Round-trip reprice on all calibrating instruments. Calendar spread no-arbitrage check. For gas: verify winter/summer spread ≥ storage cost floor. For power: verify implied clean spark spread is economically plausible. Back-test vs realised settlements. Escalate any failure before publication.

Generation spreads & cross-commodity coupling

The generation margin — what a CCGT earns to convert gas into electricity, net of carbon costs — is E.ON's primary generation economics metric. It is measured by forward spreads, not by the level of any single commodity. Power, gas, and carbon curves are not three independent outputs: they are three legs of a single generation economics surface and must be jointly governed.

Spark spread (gross)
F_power − F_gas × HR
The gross spark spread is the generation margin before carbon costs. It measures whether a gas plant earns a positive contribution margin at a given delivery period in the forward curve. If the forward spark spread > 0, the CCGT is "in the money" — it should be scheduled to dispatch. If < 0, the plant is out of merit and should not run unless there are contractual obligations.
Clean spark spread (net generation margin)
F_power − F_gas × HR − F_carbon × EF
The clean spark spread subtracts the cost of CO₂ allowances the plant must surrender. It is the true generation margin in a carbon-regulated market. This is the primary metric for E.ON's CCGT fleet. A negative clean spark spread means the plant loses money on every MWh generated and should not be dispatched except to honour contractual commitments.
Fuel-cost parity anchor — why it governs the power long end

The clean spark spread is the mechanism through which the power forward curve anchors to gas and carbon at the long end. In equilibrium, generators cannot persistently sell power at a price that makes dispatch uneconomic — the market clearing price converges to fuel cost. A power forward curve with a persistently negative clean spark spread at Cal+2 is either forecasting sustained uneconomic dispatch or is mis-marked. In either case, it requires formal documented justification before publication.

# Generation spread definitions  (all in €/MWh of electricity output)

# Spark spread (gross):
Spark          =  F_power(T)  −  F_gas(T) × HR

# Clean spark spread (net CCGT generation margin):
CSS            =  F_power(T)  −  F_gas(T) × HR  −  F_carbon(T) × EF

# Dark spread (gross — coal reference, legacy context):
Dark           =  F_power(T)  −  F_coal(T) × HR_coal

# Clean dark spread (coal net margin — market context, not active E.ON dispatch):
CDS            =  F_power(T)  −  F_coal(T) × HR_coal  −  F_carbon(T) × EF_coal

  HR         =  CCGT heat rate         (~1.92–2.04 MWh_gas/MWh_el  =  49–52% eff.)
  EF         =  CCGT emission factor   (~0.35–0.40 tCO₂/MWh_el)
  HR_coal    =  coal plant heat rate   (~2.5–3.0 MWh_coal/MWh_el  =  33–40% eff.)
  EF_coal    =  coal emission factor   (~0.85–1.0 tCO₂/MWh_el)

# Typical E.ON context — forward clean spark spread (German CCGT):
#   Q+1 to Q+4:   −5 to +15 €/MWh   (highly variable; calendar structure dominant)
#   Cal+1 to +3:  0 to +10 €/MWh    (tighter; long-run parity limits deviation)
#   Negative CSS: plant out-of-merit; uneconomic to dispatch without fixed obligations
E.ON context — coal is a reference, not an active curve
E.ON has largely exited coal generation. The dark spread and clean dark spread remain relevant as market-context metrics — they determine where coal sits in the German merit order, which affects the clearing price for gas plants. They are also required for residual legacy contract valuations. No standalone coal PFC is maintained: coal-related exposure is managed through the cross-commodity spread surface, not a dedicated coal curve team.

Governance requirement — joint curve consistency
Power, gas, and carbon curves must be reviewed together as a set, not independently. A clean spark spread that oscillates by ±5 €/MWh purely on publication-day curve re-calibration — with no underlying market move — is a signal that the curves are not jointly governed. Mandate: any curve publication that changes the clean spark spread by more than a defined tolerance at Cal+2 requires a second-line review before release.

6

Five-line summary

  • 01 A power forward curve maps future delivery dates to market-implied €/MWh prices, calibrated so it reprices every traded instrument used to build it — the no-arbitrage condition made operational in a non-storable market.
  • 02 Power is non-storable: there is no cost-of-carry to discipline the forward curve. The long end is anchored by fuel-cost parity (F_gas × HR + F_carbon × EF + O&M of the marginal CCGT), not by physical carry. Governance — parameter approval cycles, methodology notes, overlay sign-off, validation logs — does all the work that storage arbitrage does in every other commodity market.
  • 03 Construction method choice — bootstrap for the liquid near curve (EEX/ICE), spline for the seasonal mid curve (3M–2Y), factor/PCA for the sparse long end — is a governance decision, not a technical default. Seasonal shaping (base/peak to hourly decomposition) is not optional in a market that settles by the hour.
  • 04 Power, gas, and carbon curves must be jointly governed through the clean spark spread. A curve team that builds all three independently without cross-consistency checks will produce a generation margin surface that oscillates on model noise. The sign of the forward clean spark spread at Cal+2 is a primary model-validation output.
  • 05 Governance owns the parameters: θ (long-run equilibrium, anchored to fuel-cost parity), κ (mean-reversion speed, reviewed on material market structure change), seasonal Fourier coefficients (locked between review cycles), and shape model version (audited for every publication). Every published curve must be traceable to a named methodology note, frozen inputs, and a passed validation log.
Section 07 · Products
7

E.ON core products

E.ON's three primary curve exposures — power, gas, and carbon — are economically coupled through the generation margin. Each has a different physical structure, different construction approach by maturity zone, and different governance requirements. They must be managed as a system.

Scope note — what is not covered here
Crude oil and base metals are out of scope for the E.ON forward curve team. Coal is not maintained as a standalone active product — it is referenced as a spread input in Section 05C (generation spreads / clean dark spread) and for legacy contract valuations only.
01 · Power · Primary
Power / Electricity
Non-storable · E.ON primary exposure · generation + supply + networks · EPEX settlement
Curve construction
  • Near (0–3M): daily EEX settlement and ICE month-ahead for German DE-LU/AT zone. Direct bootstrap — exact reprice.
  • Mid (3M–2Y): seasonal quarterly/annual OTC swaps (EEX, ICE) for base and peak load. Best-fit solver for overlapping instruments; clamped cubic spline over seasonal pillars preserving winter/summer differential. Base/peak decomposition via temperature or load shape model — shape model version must be in the audit log.
  • Long (2Y+): Fourier-extended parametric or factor/PCA, with expert overlay anchored to fuel-cost parity F ≈ F_gas × HR + F_carbon × EF + O&M. Every parameter in this zone must be named, approved, and traceable.
  • Zones: primary is DE-LU/AT (EEX). Dutch APX, UK N2EX, and Nordic NordPool maintained as spread curves over the German anchor.
Stochastic model
  • Spot dynamics: OU with Fourier seasonal mean — dX = κ(θ(t)−X)dt + σdW, where θ(t) is the seasonal equilibrium fuel-cost-anchored at the long end.
  • Spikes: compound-Poisson jump term (J·dN) added to OU base; Gaussian-only models understate tail risk — experience in 2021/22 confirmed that parameters calibrated in normal conditions are unreliable in stress.
  • Two-factor (Schwartz-Smith style): short-term spike factor (κ_χ ~ 5–10 yr⁻¹) + slow long-term equilibrium; explains why prompt and Cal+3 prices are nearly uncorrelated.
  • Daily production mark: zoned bootstrap + seasonal spline + shape model. Not a stochastic model — a deterministic construction that exactly reprices calibrating instruments.
Key limitations
  • Non-storability: no cost-of-carry bound constrains calendar spreads. This is the fundamental reason power curve governance is more demanding than for any storable commodity.
  • Duck curve: solar generation suppresses midday EPEX prices, reshaping the intra-day profile year-on-year as solar capacity grows. Shape parameters calibrated on pre-2022 data misrepresent 2024+ intra-day structure. Review shape model when solar penetration changes by more than 5 GW in a zone.
  • Negative price events: power prices go negative on high renewable surplus. A curve that cannot represent expected negative prices in certain delivery windows is systematically mis-marking renewable-correlated positions.
  • Structural demand uncertainty: electrification of heat, transport, and industry creates long-end demand uncertainty that historical calibration cannot capture. Long-end overlays must name the electrification scenario assumed.
  • Market design risk: capacity remuneration mechanisms, renewable subsidy changes, nodal pricing discussions. Treat as named scenario sensitivities.
Key assumptions
  • Mean-reversion speed κ: 3–10 yr⁻¹; calibrate from deseasonalised spot AR(1) on weekly EPEX settlements. Half-life of 1–3 months expected for German power.
  • Long-run equilibrium θ: fuel-cost parity — F_gas(TTF) × CCGT heat rate + F_carbon(EUA) × emission factor + O&M (3–6 €/MWh). Specify the CCGT efficiency assumption (49–52% = HR 1.92–2.04) in the methodology note.
  • Seasonal harmonics: minimum 5 years of hourly EPEX data, temperature-normalised; annual (k=1) + semi-annual (k=2) + quarterly (k=3) as minimum; 3–6 harmonics typical for German power.
  • Peak/base ratio: derived from historical load profile; reviewed when net load profile changes by >5 GW in the zone. Distinguish weekday vs weekend ratio separately.
  • No storage arbitrage: document explicitly that the no-arbitrage condition for power applies only to financial products — users must not apply gas-style calendar spread bounds.
Validation
  • Round-trip reprice: all calibrating EEX and ICE futures and OTC swaps reprice to zero error (bootstrap) or within bid-ask (best-fit). Minimum required before publication.
  • Calendar-spread consistency: seasonal peaks must not imply risk-free financial arbitrage vs adjacent quarters. No physical storage enforces this — the curve build must.
  • Fuel parity check: implied clean spark spread at the long end must be economically plausible — positive for expected CCGT dispatch conditions; persistently negative requires formal justification before publication.
  • Peak/base ratio: implied ratio from shape model within historically observed range; outlier triggers shape model review before publication.
  • Zone basis: spread between DE-LU/AT and Dutch/UK/Nordic zones within historical range.
  • Back-test: rolling EPEX settlement comparison; report bias and RMSE per tenor bucket and separately for winter vs summer.
Key risk
Non-storability trap — governance is the only backstop

In every other commodity, storage arbitrage provides a floor under the forward curve. In power it does not. A power forward curve can imply calendar spreads that would be instantly arbitraged away in gas, oil, or metals — and they persist because no one can carry physical electricity across time. This means governance (approval cycles, parameter locks, overlay documentation, cross-commodity consistency checks) does all the work that physical arbitrage does in other markets. A power curve team that treats governance as a compliance overhead rather than a core modelling function will consistently publish wrong curves. This is the defining insight of the role.

02 · Gas · Core Input
Natural Gas
Storable · TTF primary hub · generation fuel · storage economics · supply procurement
Curve construction
  • Near (0–3M): day-ahead and prompt-month TTF and NCG settlement. Direct bootstrap; instruments are clean and liquid. Hub basis (TTF vs NCG) maintained as a separate spread curve.
  • Mid (3M–2Y): seasonal summer/winter gas swaps and quarterly OTC (EEX Natural Gas, ICE TTF). Best-fit solver; cubic spline preserving winter premium. Winter/summer spread must satisfy the storage-economics bound: W − S ≥ inject + withdraw + working gas financing cost — this is a hard governance constraint.
  • Long (2Y+): gas-to-power parity as a soft sanity check; factor model or expert overlay; LNG netback constraint as a soft ceiling.
  • Storage link: E.ON's gas storage assets (Rehden: ~4.2 TWh working capacity; Haidach: E.ON share) provide physical market insight for calibrating storage cost assumptions.
Stochastic model
  • Storage-constrained seasonality: winter/summer spread bounded below by documented injection + withdrawal + capital costs — a hard physical constraint, not a model parameter.
  • OU mean-reversion: long-run equilibrium anchored to long-run marginal gas supply cost (mix of Norwegian pipeline, LNG netback, remaining import routes). κ ~ 1–5 yr⁻¹ for TTF.
  • Two-factor (Gabillon-style): short-term deviation + long-term equilibrium; explains seasonal pattern and LNG regime shifts without overfitting.
  • Regime-aware: LNG import capacity and utilisation changes the effective long-run anchor; model must support scenario switching on the long-run mean.
  • Daily mark: bootstrap near end; seasonal spline mid; expert-overlay parametric long end.
Key limitations
  • Storage bound is a floor, not a cap: extreme cold events can push winter prices above any storage-derived ceiling; the model will understate peak-winter spike risk.
  • Hub fragmentation: TTF, NCG, NBP, PSV — all require separate curves; basis risk is not captured in a single-hub framework, and E.ON's multi-country supply business requires each hub to be governed.
  • LNG globalisation: the TTF long end is increasingly linked to Asian LNG spot prices, breaking historical European-only relationships. Pre-2021 calibrations embed regime assumptions that no longer hold.
  • Gas-to-power coupling: TTF gas is an input to German power fuel-cost parity — an error in the gas curve propagates directly into the power curve's long-end anchor. The two curves must be jointly reviewed.
Key assumptions
  • Seasonal structure: winter premium calibrated from ≥5 storage cycles of TTF settlement; temperature-normalised.
  • Storage economics (hard bound): injection rate, withdrawal rate, working gas capacity from published storage operator data and E.ON's own storage operational parameters. These set the no-arbitrage floor on winter/summer spread.
  • CCGT heat rate: 49–52% for German CCGT fleet; used in gas-to-power parity cross-check.
  • Hub basis: TTF→NCG spread modelled as a separate independent forward curve.
  • Long-run supply cost: weighted average of LNG netback (delivered ARA), Norwegian pipeline cost; review annually and after any major supply-route disruption.
Validation
  • Storage economics bound (hard flag): winter/summer spread ≥ documented inject + withdraw + financing cost. Violation = documented arbitrage = curve cannot publish.
  • Hub parity: TTF/NCG implied basis spread vs broker-quoted differentials; deviation beyond historical bid-ask triggers input review.
  • Gas-to-power parity: F_gas × HR + F_carbon × EF ≈ F_power at long end; systematic deviation signals cross-commodity inconsistency between gas and power teams.
  • Back-test per season: winter and summer RMSE tracked separately.
  • LNG regime check: implied long-end price vs published DES ARA LNG assessments.
Key risk
Storage arbitrage as governance anchor
Unlike power, gas storage provides a physical no-arbitrage bound. The winter/summer spread cannot persistently fall below injection + withdrawal cost without creating a profitable storage trade. Use this bound as a hard governance floor — a curve that violates it is mis-marked regardless of what the solver produced. E.ON's storage operations team is an internal reference for validating the storage cost parameters.
03 · Carbon · Compliance
Carbon / Emissions (EU ETS — EUAs)
Bankable · policy-driven · major compliance buyer · dispatch economics driver
Curve construction
  • Forward structure: EUA December futures (ICE ECX) for current and next vintages. Strip of December futures prices is the EUA forward curve.
  • Carry relationship: EUAs are bankable — F(T) ≈ S · e^(r·T). Forward prices cannot fall below this bound (no borrowing across compliance years).
  • Compliance seasonality: April 30 surrender deadline creates near-term demand visible in Q1 pricing. The only seasonal structure in EUAs comes from compliance demand.
  • Long end (beyond current Phase): Phase 5 post-2030 is entirely a policy assumption. This zone is not market-calibrated — it is a named scenario with explicit uncertainty bounds.
  • E.ON position: large net EUA buyer for compliance (generation-related CO₂ emissions); manages position to optimise purchase timing and hedge against carbon cost increases in the generation margin.
Stochastic model
  • Cost-of-carry + policy overlay: F(T) = S · e^(r·T) adjusted for expected cap tightening trajectory and MSR withdrawal schedule.
  • Regime-switching: EUA prices jump on regulatory announcements (CBAM, REPowerEU, Phase 5 design). Continuous diffusion models understate jump risk at policy announcement dates — use named scenario overlays for known decision dates.
  • No seasonal model for EUAs themselves; seasonality appears only through surrender demand.
  • Abatement cost floor for scenario analysis: marginal cost of the cheapest available abatement technology; currently 60–100 EUR/t near-term under base policy scenario.
Key limitations
  • Regulatory risk is the dominant risk: a policy decision can shift the entire forward curve instantaneously. No market model captures this — requires named scenario curves and stress tests around key policy decision dates.
  • No borrowing: EUAs cannot be borrowed across compliance years. Forward prices can only be at or above spot × e^(rT).
  • April 30 compliance bunching: demand spikes around surrender deadline create near-term price pressure not in smooth models.
  • Cross-commodity propagation: a +10 EUR/t EUA move increases CCGT cost by ~3.5–4 €/MWh and directly reduces the clean spark spread. An EUA curve update without simultaneous review of generation economics is incomplete.
  • Phase 5 uncertainty: rules post-2030 not fully determined. Any EUA curve beyond the current Phase is a named scenario, not a calibrated market price.
Key assumptions
  • Bankability: EUAs carry forward without cost beyond EUR €STR financing; no depreciation, no storage cost.
  • No borrowing constraint: F(T) ≥ S · e^(r·T) — hard bound.
  • Risk-free rate: EUR €STR; EUA market is EUR-denominated.
  • MSR withdrawal: deterministic schedule per published MSR rules; scenario sensitivity on faster/slower withdrawal.
  • Long-run carbon price: E.ON's governance-approved policy scenario value must be named in the methodology note — this is the most consequential long-run assumption in the generation economics model.
Validation
  • Cost-of-carry lower bound (hard flag): F(T) ≥ S · e^(r·T). Violation = data error or risk-free arbitrage. Hard governance flag.
  • Vintage spread vs carry: December-to-December spread between consecutive vintages should equal EUR financing cost; persistent excess signals dislocation.
  • Generation margin consistency: EUA curve update must be accompanied by a re-check of the clean spark spread. A +5 EUR/t carbon move should reduce CSS by ~1.75–2 €/MWh; inconsistency signals curves are not jointly governed.
  • Policy scenario stress: stress-test against published EU ETS reform scenarios; document results alongside base curve on each publication date.
  • Compliance date effect: Q1 pricing should reflect April 30 demand; if not, near-term curve understates demand pressure.
Key risk
Policy as a model parameter — and generation margin propagation

In every other commodity, the model parameters are observable. In carbon, the long-run price is ultimately a political variable — what policymakers decide the cap should be. The governance response: make the policy scenario explicit, name it, and run sensitivity around it rather than treating it as a calibrated constant. Additionally, every EUA curve publication must be accompanied by the implied impact on the clean spark spread. Carbon is not a standalone curve — it is the third leg of E.ON's generation economics and must be governed as such.
