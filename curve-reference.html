Section 01 · Foundations
1

What is a curve

A curve is a structured representation of the market's expectation of a price or rate at every future maturity — built so that it exactly reprices, or minimises the pricing error of, every liquid instrument used to construct it. It is the central input to valuation, risk measurement, and hedging across any asset class with forward delivery.

Job 01 — Discounting
Present value
A discount curve answers: what is a future cashflow worth today? It supplies discount factors P(t) = 1 / (1 + y(t))t — one per maturity — which convert a future payment into its present equivalent. In energy, every future-delivery cashflow is discounted on a risk-free overnight curve, regardless of the commodity it relates to.
Job 02 — Projection / Forward
Expected future price
A forward (projection) curve answers: what price does the market expect at each future delivery date? In energy this is the price forward curve (PFC) — the expected power, gas, or carbon price for delivery at each future period. This is what traders, risk systems, and valuation engines read when they look at the curve.
Core principle

A curve must reprice every instrument it was built from. If it does not, a market participant can trade simultaneously against the curve and the market for a risk-free gain. This is the no-arbitrage condition, and it governs every construction method described in this document.

What is a forward curve

A forward curve is the strip of market-implied prices for delivery at each future date, extracted from traded instruments — futures, swaps, and calendar spreads. The shape of the curve carries information about supply/demand expectations, storage economics, and seasonal patterns specific to the underlying commodity.

Shape — Contango
Forward > Spot
Deferred prices exceed nearby prices. Typical when storage is plentiful and cost-of-carry (financing, warehousing, insurance) is the dominant price driver. A market in contango implies that holding inventory is economical: traders can buy spot, store, and sell forward at a profit net of carry costs. Common in oil and natural gas during low-demand periods.
Shape — Backwardation
Forward < Spot
Nearby prices exceed deferred prices. Signals immediate supply tightness or strong near-term demand — the "convenience yield" on holding physical inventory outweighs carry costs. Power markets frequently exhibit backwardation ahead of seasonal demand peaks (winter heating, summer cooling) when generation scarcity drives prompt prices above forward expectations.
Energy-specific — Seasonality
Power and gas forward curves do not follow simple contango or backwardation — they embed a repeating seasonal structure: winter peaks for heating demand, summer peaks for cooling, weekly peaks for industrial load, and intra-day peaks for on-peak delivery windows. This seasonality is not noise to be smoothed away; it is a primary structural feature that the curve must preserve through every interpolation and extrapolation regime. A smooth curve in a seasonal market is a wrong curve.

Why we need them

A price forward curve is not a convenience — it is the methodological backbone of every function that touches multi-period price exposure. Without a consistent, governed curve, valuation is arbitrary, risk is unmeasurable, and hedging is guesswork.

  • Valuation Mark-to-market and fair-value accounting of any instrument with forward delivery requires a curve to discount future cashflows and establish the expected delivery price. Without it, every desk can use a different price and the aggregated P&L is fiction.
  • Risk Potential Future Exposure (PFE), Value-at-Risk (VaR), and scenario analysis all depend on a consistent forward price surface. DV01/price sensitivity across the curve maturity spectrum can only be computed if the curve is a coherent, differentiable structure.
  • Hedging A hedge must be sized against the same forward price used to value the position it hedges. Curve consistency ensures that a delta hedge computed from the curve actually offsets the market risk — inconsistent curves produce phantom hedges that leave residual exposure undetected.
  • Margin / Collateral Variation margin calls, initial margin requirements, and collateral disputes are all resolved against a reference forward price. Curve governance — who owns it, how it is built, how it is challenged — determines whether those disputes are resolved quickly or become operational risk events.
Definition — No-arbitrage

Arbitrage is a risk-free profit from a price inconsistency: buying and selling related instruments simultaneously to lock in a gain with no net exposure. In liquid markets, arbitrage opportunities cannot persist — participants trade them away, restoring consistency. A curve that violates no-arbitrage (e.g. implying a calendar spread that can be traded for risk-free profit) is a curve that is wrong, and any system priced against it is mis-marked.

Section 04 · Methods
4

Curve models

No single construction method is universally correct. The choice is a governance decision: each method occupies a different position on the flexibility / transparency / stability triangle and is suited to different maturity zones and liquidity regimes.

Method 01
Sequential Bootstrapping
Build the curve instrument-by-instrument from the short end outward. Each calibrating instrument is solved to exactly zero repricing error before advancing to the next maturity. Every node is independently auditable — the price at each pillar is the direct market quote with no smoothing.
Use when: near-curve (0–3 months), liquid exchange futures with clean settlement prices. Anchors are reliable; exact reprice matters for settlement and audit.
Method 02
Best-fit / Solver
Solve all curve points jointly via a nonlinear least-squares solver that minimises weighted pricing error across all instruments simultaneously. Handles overlapping instruments and interdependencies cleanly. Repricing errors are minimised but not guaranteed to be zero.
Use when: mid-curve with overlapping OTC instruments (quarterly swaps that span multiple pillars). Robustness matters more than exact audit trail.
Method 03
Spline Interpolation
Fit a smooth piecewise-polynomial (cubic or tension spline) through the calibrated pillar prices. Guarantees continuous first and second derivatives. Can produce oscillation or negative implied forward prices between sparse pillars if unconstrained.
Use when: seasonal mid-curve (3 months – 2 years) where smooth shape matters for risk aggregation. Use natural or clamped boundary conditions to suppress endpoint oscillation.
Method 04
Seasonal Shaping
Decompose base/peak calendar quotes into hourly granularity via a load or temperature model. The shape model converts aggregate quarterly anchors into the intra-period profile the market does not directly quote. Must be versioned and formally approved — it is the primary source of cross-BU price inconsistency when ungoverned.
Use when: hourly or daily granularity is required for settlement, structured product valuation, or generation dispatch optimisation.
Method 05
Factor / PCA Model
Reduce the curve to a small number of orthogonal factors (level, slope, curvature — analogous to Nelson-Siegel in rates) estimated from historical covariance. Stable and parsimonious; well-behaved extrapolation to illiquid tenors. Cannot reprice every pillar exactly; misspecification risk if seasonal structure is strong.
Use when: sparse illiquid long end (2Y+), scenario generation, or stress testing. Stability and interpretability outweigh exact calibration.

How we price commodities

The present value of a commodity position is the sum of discounted forward cashflows over every delivery period. The formula is structurally identical to a fixed-income present value — only the interpretation of the forward rate and the delivery profile changes. 

# Present value of a commodity forward position

PV  =  Σ  P(tᵢ)  ×  F(tᵢ)  ×  Qᵢ     summed over delivery periods i = 1 … N

  P(tᵢ)  =  discount factor at delivery time tᵢ  =  1 / (1 + r(tᵢ))^tᵢ
  F(tᵢ)  =  forward price for delivery at tᵢ   (from the price forward curve)
  Qᵢ     =  quantity delivered in period i      (MWh, MMBtu, tonne, barrel …)

Six factors determine whether this formula produces a number that is commercially correct or technically precise but commercially wrong:

  1. 1
    Delivery profile
    Qi is not a single number — it is a per-period quantity that reflects the actual delivery schedule: base-load vs peak-hour, weekday vs weekend, swing volume rights. Getting the profile wrong mis-sizes every hedge derived from the PV.
  2. 2
    Storage / cost-of-carry
    For storable commodities (gas, oil, metals), the forward curve embeds storage costs, financing, and the convenience yield. These must be reflected in F(ti) or stripped out before computing spread positions — failing to do so conflates carry with directional exposure.
  3. 3
    Seasonality
    F(ti) varies structurally across the year. Using a flat or linearly interpolated price between quarterly pillars ignores intra-quarter seasonal peaks and produces systematic mis-marking of peak/off-peak structured products.
  4. 4
    Cross-commodity coupling
    Power F(ti) is not independent of gas and carbon prices — spark and dark spreads link them economically. A position priced solely on the power curve without accounting for these linkages may appear hedged while carrying implicit fuel-price exposure.
  5. 5
    Volatility / optionality
    Instruments with optionality (swing contracts, tolling agreements, embedded call options) cannot be priced by the forward curve alone — they require a volatility surface or stochastic model. The PV formula above is the deterministic core; add an option-adjusted spread or run a Monte Carlo on top for optionality.
  6. 6
    Liquidity / granularity gap
    The market quotes quarterly or monthly — the position requires daily or hourly. The granularity gap between market anchor and delivery period is filled by the shape model. Any error in shaping flows directly into F(ti) and is indistinguishable from market price movement in P&L attribution.
Recurring trap — granularity mismatch
The most common pricing error in commodity desks is using a quarterly forward price as a proxy for an intra-quarter delivery. A Q1 base price applied to a January-only peak contract over-prices in February and March while under-pricing January — producing a P&L that appears flat in aggregate but oscillates within the quarter. Always decompose to the settlement granularity before discounting.

Rates → energy mapping

The multi-curve, no-arbitrage framework that became standard for interest-rate derivatives transfers directly to commodity curve construction. The table below maps each concept from its rates-world origin to its energy-world equivalent.

Rates world Energy world
Discounting curve (OIS / risk-free overnight) Discount factors applied to future-delivery cashflows — same curve, same logic; commodity is irrelevant
Projection / forward curve (term index rate) Price forward curve — the expected power, gas, or carbon price for each future delivery period
Calibrate to deposits, FRAs, futures, swaps Calibrate to exchange futures (EEX, ICE), calendar swaps, seasonal OTC quotes
Multi-curve: right curve for right purpose Multi-commodity: power, gas, carbon — distinct curves, coupled by spark/dark spreads
No-arbitrage = reprice all calibrating instruments Same: round-trip reprice test + monotonic calendar-spread test
Bootstrapping / interpolation / Nelson-Siegel Same methods applied to energy pillars, with seasonal shaping added as an extra layer
What changes in energy
The construction discipline is identical. What energy adds on top: seasonality (repeating winter/summer wave that must be preserved, not smoothed), mean reversion and spikes (power reverts to a seasonal equilibrium and spikes on scarcity — rates do not), delivery over a period (power is for a month/quarter, not a point in time, requiring decomposition to granular shape), and cross-commodity coupling (power, gas, and carbon curves are not independent).

Five-line summary

  • 01 A curve maps future maturities to market-implied prices or rates, calibrated so it reprices every liquid instrument used to build it — that reprice condition is the no-arbitrage constraint made operational.
  • 02 Every curve does two jobs: discounting (what a future cashflow is worth today) and projection (what the market expects the price to be at each future date). In energy, these are always separate curves.
  • 03 Construction method choice — bootstrapping for the liquid near curve, spline for the seasonal mid curve, factor/PCA for the sparse long end — is a governance decision, not a technical default.
  • 04 Energy curves carry everything rates curves carry, plus four additional layers: seasonality, mean reversion and spikes, delivery-over-a-period decomposition, and cross-commodity coupling through fuel spreads.
  • 05 The construction-and-governance discipline — calibrate, interpolate, validate, document — is identical across asset classes. The energy-specific behaviour is what the modelling layer adds on top of that shared foundation.
