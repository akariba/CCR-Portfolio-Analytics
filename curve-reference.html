Section 01 · Foundations
1

What is a curve

A curve is a structured representation of the market's expectation of a price or rate at every future maturity — built so that it exactly reprices, or minimises the pricing error of, every liquid instrument used to construct it. It is the central input to valuation, risk measurement, and hedging across any asset class with forward delivery.

Job 01 — Price forward curve (PFC)
Expected delivery price
The forward curve answers: what price does the market expect for commodity delivery at each future date? For power it is the expected €/MWh for base or peak delivery in each future period. For gas it is the expected €/MWh (or p/therm) at the relevant hub. For oil it is the USD/bbl for the relevant grade and delivery point. This is what traders, risk systems, valuation engines, and hedging desks read when they look at the curve — the central number in every commodity transaction.
Job 02 — Spot vs forward relationship
Cost-of-carry & market expectation
The forward price is not simply "spot plus interest". For storable commodities (gas, oil, coal, metals) it embeds the cost of physical storage, financing, and the convenience yield from holding inventory: F(T) = S · e^((r + s − y)·T). For non-storable commodities (power) there is no carry relationship — the forward price is a pure market expectation of supply-demand balance at the future delivery date, anchored to fuel-cost economics at the long end.
Core principle — commodity no-arbitrage

A curve must reprice every instrument it was built from. For storable commodities, physical storage provides a second layer of discipline: if the forward price exceeds spot plus carry costs, traders buy spot, store, and sell forward — collapsing the deviation. For non-storable commodities (power), only the financial no-arbitrage condition applies: the curve must reprice all calibrating futures and swaps, or a risk-free financial trade exists against it.

Section 02 · Foundations
2

What is a forward curve

A forward curve is the strip of market-implied prices for delivery at each future date, extracted from traded instruments — futures, swaps, and calendar spreads. The shape of the curve carries information about supply/demand expectations, storage economics, and seasonal patterns specific to the underlying commodity.

Shape — Contango
Forward > Spot
Deferred prices exceed nearby prices. Typical when storage is plentiful and cost-of-carry (financing, warehousing, insurance) is the dominant price driver. A market in contango implies that holding inventory is economical: traders can buy spot, store, and sell forward at a profit net of carry costs. Common in oil and natural gas during low-demand periods.
Shape — Backwardation
Forward < Spot
Nearby prices exceed deferred prices. Signals immediate supply tightness or strong near-term demand — the "convenience yield" on holding physical inventory outweighs carry costs. Power markets frequently exhibit backwardation ahead of seasonal demand peaks (winter heating, summer cooling) when generation scarcity drives prompt prices above forward expectations.
Energy-specific — Seasonality
Power and gas forward curves do not follow simple contango or backwardation — they embed a repeating seasonal structure: winter peaks for heating demand, summer peaks for cooling, weekly peaks for industrial load, and intra-day peaks for on-peak delivery windows. This seasonality is not noise to be smoothed away; it is a primary structural feature that the curve must preserve through every interpolation and extrapolation regime. A smooth curve in a seasonal market is a wrong curve.
Section 03 · Foundations
3

Why we need them

A price forward curve is not a convenience — it is the methodological backbone of every function that touches multi-period price exposure. Without a consistent, governed curve, valuation is arbitrary, risk is unmeasurable, and hedging is guesswork.

  • Valuation Mark-to-market and fair-value accounting of any instrument with forward delivery requires a curve to discount future cashflows and establish the expected delivery price. Without it, every desk can use a different price and the aggregated P&L is fiction.
  • Risk Potential Future Exposure (PFE), Value-at-Risk (VaR), and scenario analysis all depend on a consistent forward price surface. DV01/price sensitivity across the curve maturity spectrum can only be computed if the curve is a coherent, differentiable structure.
  • Hedging A hedge must be sized against the same forward price used to value the position it hedges. Curve consistency ensures that a delta hedge computed from the curve actually offsets the market risk — inconsistent curves produce phantom hedges that leave residual exposure undetected.
  • Margin / Collateral Variation margin calls, initial margin requirements, and collateral disputes are all resolved against a reference forward price. Curve governance — who owns it, how it is built, how it is challenged — determines whether those disputes are resolved quickly or become operational risk events.
Definition — No-arbitrage

Arbitrage is a risk-free profit from a price inconsistency: buying and selling related instruments simultaneously to lock in a gain with no net exposure. In liquid markets, arbitrage opportunities cannot persist — participants trade them away, restoring consistency. A curve that violates no-arbitrage (e.g. implying a calendar spread that can be traded for risk-free profit) is a curve that is wrong, and any system priced against it is mis-marked.

Section 04 · Methods
4

Curve models

No single construction method is universally correct. The choice is a governance decision: each method occupies a different position on the flexibility / transparency / stability triangle and is suited to different maturity zones and liquidity regimes.

Method 01
Sequential Bootstrapping
Build the curve instrument-by-instrument from the short end outward. Each calibrating instrument is solved to exactly zero repricing error before advancing to the next maturity. Every node is independently auditable — the price at each pillar is the direct market quote with no smoothing.
Use when: near-curve (0–3 months), liquid exchange futures with clean settlement prices. Anchors are reliable; exact reprice matters for settlement and audit.
Method 02
Best-fit / Solver
Solve all curve points jointly via a nonlinear least-squares solver that minimises weighted pricing error across all instruments simultaneously. Handles overlapping instruments and interdependencies cleanly. Repricing errors are minimised but not guaranteed to be zero.
Use when: mid-curve with overlapping OTC instruments (quarterly swaps that span multiple pillars). Robustness matters more than exact audit trail.
Method 03
Spline Interpolation
Fit a smooth piecewise-polynomial (cubic or tension spline) through the calibrated pillar prices. Guarantees continuous first and second derivatives. Can produce oscillation or negative implied forward prices between sparse pillars if unconstrained.
Use when: seasonal mid-curve (3 months – 2 years) where smooth shape matters for risk aggregation. Use natural or clamped boundary conditions to suppress endpoint oscillation.
Method 04
Seasonal Shaping
Decompose base/peak calendar quotes into hourly granularity via a load or temperature model. The shape model converts aggregate quarterly anchors into the intra-period profile the market does not directly quote. Must be versioned and formally approved — it is the primary source of cross-BU price inconsistency when ungoverned.
Use when: hourly or daily granularity is required for settlement, structured product valuation, or generation dispatch optimisation.
Method 05
Factor / PCA Model
Reduce the curve to a small number of orthogonal factors (level, slope, curvature — analogous to Nelson-Siegel in rates) estimated from historical covariance. Stable and parsimonious; well-behaved extrapolation to illiquid tenors. Cannot reprice every pillar exactly; misspecification risk if seasonal structure is strong.
Use when: sparse illiquid long end (2Y+), scenario generation, or stress testing. Stability and interpretability outweigh exact calibration.
Section 05 · Methods
5

How we price commodities

The present value of a commodity position is the sum of discounted forward cashflows over every delivery period. The formula is structurally identical to a fixed-income present value — only the interpretation of the forward rate and the delivery profile changes. 

# Present value of a commodity forward position

PV  =  Σ  P(tᵢ)  ×  F(tᵢ)  ×  Qᵢ     summed over delivery periods i = 1 … N

  P(tᵢ)  =  discount factor at delivery time tᵢ  =  1 / (1 + r(tᵢ))^tᵢ
  F(tᵢ)  =  forward price for delivery at tᵢ   (from the price forward curve)
  Qᵢ     =  quantity delivered in period i      (MWh, MMBtu, tonne, barrel …)

Six factors determine whether this formula produces a number that is commercially correct or technically precise but commercially wrong:

  1. 1
    Delivery profile
    Qi is not a single number — it is a per-period quantity that reflects the actual delivery schedule: base-load vs peak-hour, weekday vs weekend, swing volume rights. Getting the profile wrong mis-sizes every hedge derived from the PV.
  2. 2
    Storage / cost-of-carry
    For storable commodities (gas, oil, metals), the forward curve embeds storage costs, financing, and the convenience yield. These must be reflected in F(ti) or stripped out before computing spread positions — failing to do so conflates carry with directional exposure.
  3. 3
    Seasonality
    F(ti) varies structurally across the year. Using a flat or linearly interpolated price between quarterly pillars ignores intra-quarter seasonal peaks and produces systematic mis-marking of peak/off-peak structured products.
  4. 4
    Cross-commodity coupling
    Power F(ti) is not independent of gas and carbon prices — spark and dark spreads link them economically. A position priced solely on the power curve without accounting for these linkages may appear hedged while carrying implicit fuel-price exposure.
  5. 5
    Volatility / optionality
    Instruments with optionality (swing contracts, tolling agreements, embedded call options) cannot be priced by the forward curve alone — they require a volatility surface or stochastic model. The PV formula above is the deterministic core; add an option-adjusted spread or run a Monte Carlo on top for optionality.
  6. 6
    Liquidity / granularity gap
    The market quotes quarterly or monthly — the position requires daily or hourly. The granularity gap between market anchor and delivery period is filled by the shape model. Any error in shaping flows directly into F(ti) and is indistinguishable from market price movement in P&L attribution.
Recurring trap — granularity mismatch
The most common pricing error in commodity desks is using a quarterly forward price as a proxy for an intra-quarter delivery. A Q1 base price applied to a January-only peak contract over-prices in February and March while under-pricing January — producing a P&L that appears flat in aggregate but oscillates within the quarter. Always decompose to the settlement granularity before discounting.

Expected future price — formulas & methods

The expected future commodity price at maturity T is the market's best estimate of where the spot price will be when delivery occurs. For storable commodities it is anchored by the cost of physically carrying inventory to that date. For non-storable commodities it is a pure supply-demand expectation. The formulas below show how to calculate it for each case and how all the layers — carry, mean-reversion, seasonality, and delivery profile — combine into the full forward price used in valuation and hedging.

Storable commodities (gas, oil, coal, metals)
The expected delivery price is anchored by storage economics: F(T) = S₀ · e^((r+s−y)·T). Physical arbitrage enforces this — if the forward diverges from carry cost, traders buy spot, store, and sell forward until it corrects.
Non-storable commodities (power)
No cost-of-carry exists. The expected delivery price is a market consensus on future supply/demand balance, anchored at the long end by fuel-cost economics: F ≈ gas × heat_rate + carbon × EF + O&M. No physical arbitrage enforces it — governance does.
Eq 01 Cost-of-carry forward price (storable commodities)

For any commodity that can be stored, the no-arbitrage forward price is fully determined by the cost of buying spot and holding it to delivery. Any deviation creates a cash-and-carry arbitrage that is immediately traded away in liquid markets. 

F(0, T)  =  S₀  ·  e^((r + s − y) · T)

  S₀  =  spot price today
  r   =  risk-free rate (continuously compounded, same currency as commodity)
  s   =  storage cost rate (per year: warehousing, insurance, financing of physical)
  y   =  convenience yield (per year: benefit of holding physical vs paper)
  T   =  time to delivery in years
Contango condition (r + s > y)
When storage costs exceed the convenience yield, forward prices exceed spot. Buying spot and selling forward earns the carry. Prevalent in oil/metals during inventory surpluses.
Backwardation condition (y > r + s)
When convenience yield dominates, forward prices fall below spot. Physical holders earn a scarcity premium — "convenience" — that outweighs carry costs. Prevalent in tight markets.
Discrete compounding equivalent

For annual compounding: F(0,T) = S₀ · (1 + r + s − y)^T. The continuous form is standard for derivatives; the discrete form is used in some physical commodity markets. Never mix the two conventions within a single curve.

Eq 02 Non-storable commodity — direct calibration (power)

For power, storage is impossible. The cost-of-carry formula does not apply. The forward price cannot be derived from spot — it must be read directly from market quotes or anchored to fundamental economics. The no-arbitrage condition operates through financial instruments only, not through physical carry. 

# Power: no cost-of-carry relationship exists.
# Forward price is either directly market-quoted or anchored to fuel cost parity:

F_power(T)  ≈  F_gas(T) × HR  +  F_carbon(T) × EF  +  OM  +  capacity_premium(T)

  HR    =  heat rate of the marginal generator (typically CCGT, ~49–52%)
  EF    =  emission factor (tCO₂/MWh, ~0.35–0.40 for CCGT)
  OM    =  variable O&M cost of the marginal generator (€/MWh)
  capacity_premium  =  scarcity premium above fuel cost; zero in normal conditions

# Use this as a long-end anchor / sanity check, not as the primary construction method.
Eq 03 Mean-reverting expected price — Ornstein-Uhlenbeck

Mean reversion is the defining dynamic of energy and commodity prices. The OU process models the speed at which prices return to a long-run equilibrium after a shock — and its expected value formula is the mathematical expression of that reversion. 

# Stochastic differential equation (continuous time):
dX  =  κ · (θ(t) − X) · dt  +  σ · dW

  κ     =  mean-reversion speed (yr⁻¹); power: 3–10; gas: 1–5; oil: 0.5–2
  θ(t)  =  seasonal equilibrium at time t  (see Eq 05 for seasonal form)
  σ     =  volatility of the spot process
  dW    =  standard Brownian increment

# Closed-form expected value at horizon T:
E[X(T)]  =  θ  +  (X₀ − θ) · e^(−κ · T)

# As T → ∞,  E[X(T)] → θ  (long-run equilibrium)
# As T → 0,  E[X(T)] → X₀  (today's spot)
# Half-life of a shock: T½ = ln(2) / κ   (time for 50% of shock to dissipate)

# Discrete approximation for numerical bootstrapping (Euler-Maruyama):
X(t+Δt)  ≈  X(t)  +  κ · (θ − X(t)) · Δt  +  σ · √Δt · ε     ε ~ N(0,1)

Calibrate κ: regress deseasonalised spot returns on lagged level → AR(1) coefficient φ = e^(−κΔt), so κ = −ln(φ)/Δt. A weekly calibration (Δt = 1/52) on power residuals typically yields κ ∈ [3, 8].

Eq 04 Two-factor expected price — Schwartz-Smith decomposition

The two-factor model separates log-spot into a mean-reverting short-term deviation and a random-walk long-term equilibrium. It explains the empirical observation that front-month and back-of-curve prices are nearly uncorrelated — they are driven by different factors with different speeds and different risk premia. 

# Decompose log spot price into two latent factors:
ln S(t)  =  χ(t)  +  ξ(t)

  χ(t)  =  short-term deviation  →  mean-reverts to zero at speed κ_χ
  ξ(t)  =  long-term equilibrium →  random walk with drift μ_ξ

# Factor dynamics:
dχ  =  −κ_χ · χ · dt  +  σ_χ · dW₁
dξ  =  μ_ξ · dt    +  σ_ξ · dW₂      Corr(dW₁, dW₂) = ρ

# Expected commodity price at horizon T:
E[χ(T)]     =  χ₀ · e^(−κ_χ · T)             short-term factor decays to zero as T → ∞
E[ξ(T)]     =  ξ₀ + μ_ξ · T                long-term equilibrium grows at rate μ_ξ
E[ln S(T)]  =  χ₀ · e^(−κ_χT) + ξ₀ + μ_ξT

# Observed forward price — calibrate by matching the model to the market futures strip:
ln F(0,T)  =  χ₀·e^(−κ_χT) + ξ₀ + μ_ξT + variance_correction(T)

  variance_correction(T) = ½[σ_χ²(1−e^(−2κT))/(2κ) + σ_ξ²T + 2ρσ_χσ_ξ(1−e^(−κT))/κ]
  This Jensen's inequality correction converts E[ln S] to ln E[S] for a log-normal process.

Practical use: calibrate {κ_χ, σ_χ, σ_ξ, μ_ξ, ρ} via Kalman filter on the historical commodity futures strip. The term structure of futures volatility (decreasing with maturity) is the key identifying feature of the short-term factor.

Eq 05 Seasonal expected price — Fourier equilibrium

For energy commodities, the long-run equilibrium θ is not a constant — it is a deterministic seasonal function of time. Separating the deterministic seasonal component from the stochastic residual is the essential first step before calibrating any mean-reversion or volatility parameter. 

# Additive seasonal mean-reversion model:
E[S(T)]  =  θ(T)  +  (S₀ − θ(0)) · e^(−κ · T)

# Fourier seasonal equilibrium (annual + sub-annual cycles):
θ(T)  =  α₀  +  Σₖ  [aₖ · cos(2πkT/P)  +  bₖ · sin(2πkT/P)]

  α₀     =  long-run mean price level
  aₖ,bₖ =  Fourier coefficients for harmonic k  (estimated by OLS on historical data)
  P      =  period: 1 year for annual cycle, 1/52 for weekly, 1/365 for intra-day
  k      =  harmonic number; k=1 gives fundamental cycle, k=2 semi-annual, etc.

# Amplitude and phase of each harmonic:
Amplitudeₖ  =  √(aₖ² + bₖ²)
Phaseₖ      =  arctan(bₖ / aₖ)        tells you when the seasonal peak occurs

# Multiplicative alternative (log-space, avoids negative prices):
ln θ(T)  =  ln α₀  +  Σₖ  [aₖ · cos(2πkT/P)  +  bₖ · sin(2πkT/P)]

Governance note: the Fourier coefficients {aₖ, bₖ} are model parameters — they must be locked between approval cycles, documented with the number of harmonics used, the historical estimation window, and any temperature-normalisation applied. A change to any coefficient is a methodology change requiring formal sign-off.

Eq 06 Commodity hedging pressure & forward price bias

In commodity markets, producers (miners, generators, farmers) systematically sell forward to lock in revenue, creating excess supply of forward contracts relative to demand. Buyers of that forward risk earn a premium — the forward price tends to trade below the expected delivery price for short-dated contracts (Keynes' "normal backwardation"). For long-dated contracts or buyer-hedging-dominated markets, the sign reverses. 

# Commodity forward price premium (Keynes normal backwardation):
F(0,T)  =  E[S(T)]  −  π(T)

  E[S(T)]  =  market's best estimate of spot at delivery (fundamental value)
  π(T)     =  hedging premium paid by producers to buyers of forward price risk

# When producers dominate hedging (generators, oil majors, miners):
  π(T) > 0  →  forward price BELOW expected spot  (buyers earn a premium)

# When consumers dominate hedging (utilities buying gas, manufacturers buying metals):
  π(T) < 0  →  forward price ABOVE expected spot  (sellers earn a premium)

# Empirical direction by commodity (indicative, front months):
  Power:        forward < expected spot  (generators are net short hedgers)
  Natural gas:  forward < expected spot  (producers are net short hedgers)
  Crude oil:    near-zero bias           (balanced hedging both sides)
  Base metals:  sign varies by cycle     (miners vs industrial consumers)
  EUAs:         forward ≥ expected spot  (compliance buyers create demand pressure)
Curve construction implication

For valuation and mark-to-market, always use the observed forward price from the market — it is the fair contractual value regardless of any hedging premium. For scenario generation and fundamental price forecasting, you may want the expected spot, which requires adding the estimated hedging premium back to the forward price. The two are different numbers for different purposes — always be explicit about which one you are using.

Eq 08 The complete forward price — all adjustments combined

In practice the forward price at any maturity combines cost-of-carry, mean-reversion dynamics, seasonality, and risk premium. The full expression for an energy forward curve makes each layer explicit and shows where each governance parameter lives. 

# Full forward price at maturity T (energy, storable with slow storage like gas):

F(T)  =   [ θ  +  (S₀ − θ) · e^(−κT) ]    ← mean reversion to long-run level θ
        ×  seasonal(T)                  ← Fourier seasonal adjustment
        ×  e^((r + s − y)T)            ← cost of carry (if storable)
        ×  shape(T)                    ← intra-period delivery profile (peak vs base)

# Each layer maps to a governance parameter:
  θ, κ         →  mean-reversion methodology note  (review trigger: structural market change)
  seasonal(T)  →  shape model methodology note     (review trigger: generation mix change)
  r, s, y      →  curve inputs register            (review trigger: storage tariff, rate change)
  shape(T)     →  shaping model approval record    (review trigger: load profile shift)
Method Step-by-step: building the expected future price curve

A practical, ordered procedure applicable to any energy commodity.

  1. Bootstrap the observable near end. Take exchange-quoted futures for the first 1–3 months. Assign F(tᵢ) = market quote directly. No model, no assumption — exact reprice is the standard.
  2. Estimate the long-run equilibrium θ. For storable commodities: long-run marginal cost of supply. For power: fuel-cost parity (gas × heat rate + carbon × EF + O&M). Document the source and review frequency.
  3. Calibrate mean-reversion speed κ. Regress deseasonalised spot price changes on lagged level: ΔX_t = −κ·X_{t-1}·Δt + ε. Report the estimated half-life T½ = ln(2)/κ alongside κ.
  4. Extract and lock seasonal parameters. Regress historical spot on Fourier basis features for annual/weekly cycles. Lock the coefficients as governance parameters. Document the estimation window and temperature normalisation used.
  5. Compute E[S(T)] = θ(T) + (S₀ − θ(0)) · e^(−κT) at every required delivery horizon to produce the model-implied expected future price.
  6. Apply cost-of-carry adjustment (storable only). Multiply by e^((r+s−y)T) using current risk-free rate, documented storage cost, and calibrated convenience yield.
  7. Decompose to delivery granularity. Apply shape model to convert base/quarterly pillars into intra-period hourly or daily expected prices. Record the shape model version in the audit log.
  8. Validate. Run round-trip reprice on all calibrating instruments. Check no-arbitrage (calendar spread monotonicity, cost-of-carry bounds). Back-test rolling 3/6/12-month horizon against realised settlements. Escalate any failure before publication.

Section 06 · Reference
6

Five-line summary

  • 01 A commodity price forward curve maps future delivery maturities to market-implied prices, calibrated so it reprices every liquid exchange-quoted instrument used to build it — that reprice condition is the no-arbitrage constraint made operational.
  • 02 The price forward curve is the single output: the expected commodity price at each future delivery date. For storable commodities it is bounded by the cost-of-carry relationship; for non-storables such as power it is anchored by fuel-cost parity.
  • 03 Construction method choice — bootstrapping for the liquid near curve, spline for the seasonal mid curve, factor/PCA for the sparse long end — is a governance decision, not a technical default.
  • 04 Commodity curves require four layers beyond simple interpolation: seasonality (repeating winter/summer wave that must be preserved), mean reversion and spikes (fast-reverting spot with scarcity spikes), delivery-over-a-period decomposition (base/peak into hourly shape), and cross-commodity coupling through fuel spreads.
  • 05 Governance owns the parameters — θ, κ, seasonal harmonics, shape model version, convenience yield — not the build system. Every published curve must be traceable to an approved methodology note, a set of frozen inputs, and a passed validation log.
Section 07 · Products
7

Commodity products

Each commodity class has a different physical structure, a different liquidity profile, and a different dominant price driver — and those differences determine which curve model is appropriate, what starting assumptions are valid, and which validation checks can actually catch a bad curve before it reaches production.

01 · Power
Power / Electricity
Non-storable · mean-reverting · strong multi-scale seasonality · spike risk on scarcity
Calculation approach
  • Near (0–3M): direct bootstrap from exchange day-ahead and month-ahead futures (EEX, ICE). Step-function reprice — exact by construction.
  • Mid (3M–2Y): calibrate seasonal quarterly/annual OTC swaps; decompose base/peak via a load or temperature shape model. Clamped cubic spline over seasonal pillars, preserving winter/summer differentials.
  • Long (2Y+): Fourier-extended parametric model (Nelson-Siegel + seasonal terms) or factor/PCA. Expert overlay anchored to fuel-cost parity: F ≈ gas × heat_rate + carbon × EF + O&M.
Best model
  • Spot dynamics: Ornstein-Uhlenbeck with Fourier seasonal mean — dX = κ(θ(t)−X)dt + σdW where θ(t) is a seasonal equilibrium.
  • Spikes: add compound-Poisson jump term (J·dN); Gaussian-only models understate tail risk.
  • Two-factor (Schwartz-Smith style): short-term spike factor + long-term equilibrium; explains why front/back decorrelate.
  • For the daily production mark: zoned bootstrap + spline + seasonal shape — not a full stochastic model.
Limitations
  • Non-storability: no cost-of-carry constraint; the curve can be internally inconsistent without a physical correction mechanism to force it back.
  • Spike risk: extreme scarcity events make historical calibration of κ and σ unreliable; parameters estimated in normal regimes understate crisis tails.
  • Shape uncertainty: base/peak decomposition is model-dependent; ungoverned seasonality is the primary source of cross-BU price inconsistency.
  • Structural change: renewable penetration reshapes the intra-day profile (duck curve) over time; parameters calibrated on 5-year history may already be stale.
Starting assumptions
  • Mean-reversion speed κ: typically 3–10 yr⁻¹ (fast); calibrate from deseasonalised residuals via AR(1).
  • Long-run equilibrium: fuel-cost stack — gas forward × heat rate + carbon × emission factor + fixed O&M.
  • Seasonality: ≥5 years of hourly data, temperature-normalised; annual + weekly + intra-day Fourier terms.
  • Peak/base ratio: derived from historical load profile; reviewed when generation mix changes by >5% of installed capacity.
  • No storage arbitrage: power is non-storable — the no-arbitrage condition applies only to financial products, not to physical delivery.
Validation methods
  • Round-trip reprice: all calibrating exchange futures and OTC swaps must reprice to zero error (bootstrap) or within bid-ask (best-fit).
  • Calendar-spread monotonicity: seasonal peaks must not imply risk-free arbitrage vs adjacent quarters.
  • Fuel parity check: implied clean spark spread at long end must be economically plausible (≥0 for expected generation margin).
  • Peak/base ratio: implied ratio must sit within observable historical range; outliers trigger shape model review.
  • Back-test: rolling comparison of curve at construction date vs subsequently settled prompt prices; report bias and RMSE per tenor bucket.
ⓘ Key risk
Non-storability trap

In every other commodity, storage arbitrage provides a floor under the forward curve. In power it does not. A power forward curve can imply calendar spreads that would be arbitraged away in any storable market — and they persist because no one can carry physical power across time. This means governance (approval cycles, parameter locks, overlay documentation) does all the work that physical arbitrage does in other markets.

02 · Gas
Natural Gas
Storable · seasonal · hub-fragmented · storage economics define winter/summer spread
Calculation approach
  • Near (0–3M): day-ahead and prompt-month TTF/NCG/NBP settlement. Direct bootstrap; instruments are clean and liquid.
  • Mid (3M–2Y): seasonal summer/winter swaps and quarterly OTC. Cubic spline preserving winter premium; summer/winter spread must satisfy storage economics: W−S ≥ inject + withdraw + capital.
  • Long (2Y+): gas-to-power parity (dark spread) + LNG netback constraint as a soft ceiling. Factor model or expert overlay; this is the most governance-intensive zone.
  • Hub basis curves (TTF vs NCG, TTF vs NBP) built separately and added as spread adjustments.
Best model
  • Gabillon two-factor: short-term spot deviation + long-term equilibrium, both mean-reverting; explains seasonal pattern without overfit.
  • Storage-constrained seasonality: winter/summer spread bounded below by injection + withdrawal costs; bound is a hard constraint, not a model parameter.
  • Regime-aware: LNG import/export regimes change the effective long-run anchor; model should support scenario switching on long-run mean.
  • For the daily mark: bootstrap near end; seasonal spline mid; expert-overlay-adjusted parametric long end.
Limitations
  • Storage bound is a floor, not a cap: extreme weather can push winter prices above any storage-implied ceiling; the model will understate peak-winter risk.
  • Hub fragmentation: TTF, NCG, NBP, PSV, TTF/NBP basis all require separate curves; basis risk is not captured in a single-curve framework.
  • LNG globalisation: the long end is increasingly linked to global LNG pricing, breaking historical TTF-anchored relationships.
  • Within-day gap: curve is built on day-ahead settlement but delivery is intraday; intra-day spread is unobserved and unhedgeable from the forward curve alone.
Starting assumptions
  • Seasonal structure: winter premium calibrated from ≥5 storage cycles; normalise for above/below-average temperature years.
  • Storage parameters: injection rate, withdrawal rate, working capacity — these set the no-arbitrage bounds on the winter/summer spread.
  • Gas heat rate: 49–52% for CCGT; used in gas-to-power parity constraint.
  • Basis differentials: TTF→NCG, TTF→NBP spreads treated as independent forward curves; do not embed in the main TTF curve.
  • LNG parity: long end bounded softly by delivered LNG cost (subject to regasification capacity and shipping); treat as a stress scenario, not a hard constraint.
Validation methods
  • Storage economics test: winter/summer spread ≥ documented injection + withdrawal + capital cost; violation is a hard arbitrage flag.
  • Hub parity: implied basis spread vs broker-quoted hub differentials; deviation beyond historical bid-ask triggers an input-quote review.
  • Fuel parity: gas forward × heat rate + carbon cost ≈ power forward; systematic deviation signals cross-commodity inconsistency.
  • Back-test per season: winter and summer RMSE tracked separately; seasonal bias more informative than a full-year average error.
  • Overlay frequency monitor: high long-end overlay frequency signals the model is chasing noise — triggers a formal methodology review.
ⓘ Key risk
Storage arbitrage as governance anchor
Unlike power, gas storage provides a physical no-arbitrage bound. The winter/summer spread cannot persistently fall below injection + withdrawal cost without creating a profitable storage trade. Use this bound as a hard governance check — a curve that violates it is mis-marked regardless of what the solver produced.
03 · Oil
Crude Oil & Oil Products
Highly storable · cost-of-carry dominant · convenience yield driven by inventory levels
Calculation approach
  • Near (0–6M): prompt monthly Brent/WTI (ICE/CME) and refined products (gasoil, jet, RBOB). Exact bootstrap; instruments are liquid and well-observed.
  • Mid (6M–3Y): monthly and quarterly calendar swaps. Spline interpolation; cost-of-carry relationship disciplines the shape: F(T) = S · e^((r+s−y)T).
  • Long (3Y+): convenience yield model with mean-reversion of the spot price to long-run marginal cost. Parametric extrapolation (NS or power-law decay of convenience yield).
  • Crack spread curves (gasoil/crude, jet/crude) built as separate differential curves, not embedded in the flat-price curve.
Best model
  • Gibson-Schwartz two-factor: spot price (mean-reverting) + stochastic convenience yield (also mean-reverting); explains the term structure of futures volatility and contango/backwardation transitions.
  • Schwartz-Smith: short-term deviation from equilibrium + long-term equilibrium with drift; well-calibrated to historical Brent/WTI strip data.
  • For the daily mark: bootstrap near end directly from CME/ICE settlements; spline over monthly pillars; parametric beyond 3Y.
  • Convenience yield observable from the cash-to-3M spread; use as an input, not an output.
Limitations
  • Convenience yield is unobservable beyond the front of the curve; at long tenors it must be assumed, not calibrated — the main source of long-end model risk.
  • Geopolitical discontinuities: OPEC production decisions and supply shocks create discrete jumps not captured by continuous diffusion. Historical calibration over-weights normal regimes.
  • Crack spread volatility: crude and product curves are linked but crack spreads are independently volatile; a flat-price model that ignores this understates product-specific risk.
  • Mean-reversion speed to long-run cost uncertain: historical range is 5–15 years; long-end sensitivity to this assumption is very high.
Starting assumptions
  • Storage cost: ~0.5–1.0 USD/bbl/month (tanker storage); verifiable from published tanker rates.
  • Risk-free rate: USD SOFR; oil markets are USD-denominated.
  • Convenience yield: calibrated from near-term calendar spread; typically 0–10% annualised; strongly negative in contango (inventory surplus), zero/positive in backwardation.
  • Long-run marginal cost: 60–80 USD/bbl for conventional production (cost-curve dependent; review annually with IEA/EIA supply-cost data).
  • Correlation crude/products: ~0.85–0.95 for gasoil/crude; crack spreads modelled as residuals on a separate curve.
Validation methods
  • Cost-of-carry bound: F(T) ≤ S · e^((r+s)T); if breached, a documented storage arbitrage exists — hard flag.
  • Crack spread consistency: implied gasoil/crude forward ratio within historical crack spread range (typically 10–25 USD/bbl); outlier triggers product-curve review.
  • Convenience yield vs inventory: implied convenience yield should be negatively correlated with published inventory levels (EIA weekly, IEA monthly); divergence signals model-input error.
  • DV01 decay: price sensitivity per pillar should decrease monotonically with maturity for a cost-of-carry curve; flat or rising DV01 at long end signals over-reliance on parametric extrapolation.
  • Calendar spread vs storage cost documentation: near-term spread deviation beyond documented storage cost must be escalated before publication.
ⓘ Key risk
Regime shift risk

Oil markets alternate between contango (inventory surplus, cost-of-carry dominates) and backwardation (inventory deficit, convenience yield dominates). The transition between these regimes is regime-like — the forward curve shape flips quickly when OPEC cuts production or a major supply disruption occurs. A model calibrated in one regime will misjudge the other. Governance response: run both regimes as named scenarios and flag when the market is transitioning.

04 · Coal
Thermal Coal
Storable · freight-embedded · structurally declining demand in carbon-regulated markets
Calculation approach
  • Near (0–6M): API2 (ARA CIF) and API4 (Richards Bay FOB) index from ICE/Platts. Monthly bootstrap; instruments are reasonably liquid in prompt months.
  • Mid (6M–2Y): seasonal and annual OTC swaps; OTC market is thinner than gas/power — best-fit solver preferred over sequential bootstrap to handle overlapping instruments.
  • Long (2Y+): supply-side anchor: long-run marginal cost of seaborne thermal coal + freight. Expert overlay dominates; every parameter must be documented.
  • API2/API4 spread modelled as a freight + quality differential curve; built and governed separately from the flat-price coal curve.
Best model
  • Cost-of-carry with quality adjustment: simpler dynamics than gas or power; seasonality is mild relative to temperature-driven commodities.
  • Mean-reversion to marginal supply cost: long-run anchor is the marginal cost of extraction + freight + quality premium; reversion is slower than gas (1–3 yr⁻¹ typical).
  • No seasonal model required for flat-price coal; seasonality enters through demand (winter heating) and is observable in the OTC swap strip rather than requiring a shape model.
  • API2/API4 basis: model as an independent mean-reverting spread; freight and quality adjustments applied as additive overlays.
Limitations
  • Thin long-end liquidity: the OTC market beyond 2Y is illiquid; long-end curve is essentially expert overlay with a market anchor at 2Y.
  • Quality heterogeneity: API2 is for 6,000 kcal/kg NAR standard specification; physical delivery involves quality adjustment factors not captured in the forward price.
  • Carbon dependency: clean dark spread (coal-to-power profitability net of carbon) drives demand; the coal curve cannot be governed independently of the EUA curve — a 10 EUR/t carbon move is a direct coal demand shock.
  • Freight risk embedded: API2 is CIF ARA — freight cost embedded in the price; changes in Baltic/Capesize rates affect the curve independently of commodity value.
  • Structural demand decline: European coal demand is in secular decline due to carbon pricing; long-end assumptions about demand levels become stale faster than in other markets.
Starting assumptions
  • Standard specification: API2 = API4 = 6,000 kcal/kg NAR; any non-standard coal requires an explicit calorific value adjustment factor.
  • Freight baseline: benchmark Capesize rate (Baltic Exchange); treat as a separate risk factor, not embedded in the commodity price model.
  • Carbon pass-through: assume 100% carbon cost pass-through to the dark spread; if the regulatory regime changes this assumption, it must be updated formally.
  • Long-run supply cost: 60–90 USD/t for seaborne thermal coal; derived from published cost-curve data (IEA, Wood Mackenzie); review annually.
  • Demand trajectory: declining in Europe; long-end curve must reflect structural demand decline in line with agreed policy scenario.
Validation methods
  • Clean dark spread: F_coal × heat_rate − F_carbon × EF ≥ 0 at the long end for coal to remain in the dispatch stack; violation signals that carbon assumptions are inconsistent with power/coal curves.
  • API2/API4 spread: implied basis must sit within historical freight + quality differential range; outlier triggers freight-model review.
  • Back-test vs ICE/Platts settlements: rolling RMSE per maturity bucket; persistent bias in the 1–2Y zone signals OTC liquidity deterioration not reflected in instrument weighting.
  • Expert overlay log: every long-end overlay must be documented with a named rationale (fleet retirement schedule, policy scenario, demand outlook); undocumented overlays are a governance violation.
  • Liquidity flag: instruments used beyond 2Y must carry a low-liquidity flag; confidence intervals widen explicitly in the documentation.
ⓘ Key risk
Cross-commodity dependency
A coal curve that is not jointly governed with the carbon curve will produce internally inconsistent clean dark spreads. Any scenario where carbon rises sharply must simultaneously update the coal curve's implied demand assumptions — failing to do so produces a coal curve that implies perpetual uneconomic coal generation, which is both commercially wrong and a governance red flag.
05 · Carbon
Carbon / Emissions (EU ETS — EUAs)
Bankable · policy-driven · one-directional carry · compliance deadline seasonality
Calculation approach
  • Near curve: EUA December futures (ICE ECX) for current and next vintage. Annual vintages are the primary instruments; strip of December prices is the forward curve.
  • Forward structure: EUAs are bankable (carry forward without cost beyond financing), so the dominant relationship is: F(T) ≈ S · e^(r·T) — a pure cost-of-carry curve in normal conditions.
  • Compliance seasonality: near-term curve reflects April 30 compliance demand — a predictable annual demand spike that slightly elevates prices in Q1.
  • Long end (beyond current Phase): this is entirely policy assumption — cap trajectory, MSR withdrawal rules, and abatement cost curve; not a market-calibrated zone.
Best model
  • Cost-of-carry + policy overlay: F(T) = S · e^(r·T) adjusted for expected cap tightening trajectory and MSR withdrawal schedule.
  • Regime-switching: EUA prices jump on regulatory announcements (CBAM, REPowerEU, Phase 5 design); a continuous diffusion model will systematically understate jump risk at announcement dates.
  • No seasonal model for EUAs themselves; seasonality appears only through demand (power/industrial activity drives surrender demand) — do not impose a seasonal shape on the EUA curve itself.
  • For scenario analysis: abatement cost curve model — marginal cost of the cheapest available abatement technology sets a floor under the long-run EUA price.
Limitations
  • Regulatory risk is the dominant risk and is fundamentally non-market: a policy decision can shift the entire curve instantaneously; no market model captures this.
  • No borrowing: unlike most financial instruments, EUAs cannot be borrowed across compliance years (EU ETS rules); the forward curve can only be above spot × e^(rT), never below.
  • Compliance bunching: large voluntary demand around April 30 creates near-term demand spikes not captured by continuous models; underestimated in Q1 pricing.
  • Phase boundary uncertainty: Phase 5 rules (post-2030) are not fully determined; any long-end curve beyond 2030 is a named scenario, not a calibrated market price.
Starting assumptions
  • Bankability: EUAs carry forward without cost (beyond EUR financing rate); no depreciation, no storage cost.
  • No borrowing: forward prices cannot fall below S · e^(r·T); any quote below this is either a data error or a structural trade with non-standard settlement.
  • Risk-free rate: EUR €STR (risk-free overnight); EUA market is EUR-denominated.
  • Long-run price: marginal abatement cost of the technology that clears the cap trajectory; currently 60–150 EUR/t depending on the policy scenario.
  • MSR withdrawal: modelled as a known deterministic schedule per published MSR rules; scenario sensitivity run on both faster and slower withdrawal assumptions.
Validation methods
  • Cost-of-carry lower bound: F(T) ≥ S · e^(r·T) — any violation is either a data error or implies a risk-free arbitrage (buy spot, sell forward, finance at risk-free rate); hard governance flag.
  • Vintage spread vs carry cost: the December-to-December spread between consecutive vintages should equal the EUR financing cost; persistent excess signals market dislocation or input error.
  • Phase transition test: curve must not implicitly price banking across Phase 4/5 boundary at rates inconsistent with regulatory rules.
  • Policy scenario stress: curve must be explicitly stress-tested against published EU ETS reform scenarios (REPowerEU, Fit-for-55, delayed reform); results documented alongside base curve.
  • Compliance date effect: near-term Q1 pricing should reflect April 30 compliance demand; if it does not, the curve may be understating near-term demand pressure.
ⓘ Key risk
Policy as a model parameter

In every other commodity, the model parameters are physical or financial quantities that can be observed or estimated from data. In carbon, the long-run price is ultimately a political variable — it is what policymakers decide the cap should be. The governance response is to make the policy assumption explicit, name it, and run scenarios around it rather than burying it inside a model parameter that looks like a calibrated constant.

06 · Metals
Base Metals — LME (Copper, Aluminium, Zinc, Nickel)
Highly storable · LME prompt-date convention · lease rate as convenience yield proxy
Calculation approach
  • Near (cash to 3M): LME prompt-date strip — every business day is a delivery date; a daily bootstrap is required, not a monthly one. Cash price + daily forward differentials from broker quotes.
  • Mid (3M–15M): LME monthly third-Wednesday prompts out to 15/16 months. Monthly bootstrap; cost-of-carry disciplines the shape.
  • Long (15M–3Y+): annual carries from LME or OTC market-makers. Cost-of-carry model with mean-reverting convenience yield; parametric extrapolation beyond 2Y with long-run marginal cost anchor.
  • LME lease rate curve provides an observable proxy for convenience yield and should be used as a calibration input, not a model output.
Best model
  • Gibson-Schwartz two-factor: spot price (mean-reverting) + stochastic convenience yield; explains both the term structure and the correlation between nearby and deferred prices.
  • LME lease rate as convenience yield input: F(T) = S · e^((r + s − y)T) where y = lease rate + any additional scarcity premium.
  • Schwartz-Smith as an alternative: short-term deviation + long-term equilibrium; calibrates well to historical LME copper and aluminium strips.
  • For the daily mark: bootstrap the LME prompt-date strip directly; parametric extrapolation beyond 2Y anchored to published long-run marginal cost estimates.
Limitations
  • LME prompt-date convention: the non-standard daily time axis in the first 3 months requires explicit handling; a monthly bootstrap will miss the daily forward structure and produce errors in near-term hedges.
  • Off-warrant stocks are unobserved: LME registered stocks are a subset of global inventory; off-warrant metal affects convenience yield without appearing in public data — the model observes less than it models.
  • Squeeze risk: when a single party holds a dominant long position, the cash-to-3M spread can blow out far beyond cost-of-carry bounds (LME squeeze events); the curve becomes uninformative during a squeeze.
  • Regime transitions: the move from surplus (contango) to deficit (backwardation) is rapid and regime-like; parameters calibrated in one regime will systematically mis-estimate in the other.
Starting assumptions
  • Storage cost: ~0.5–1.0 USD/t/month for LME-registered metal (warehouse rent + insurance + financing); published in LME tariff schedule.
  • Risk-free rate: USD SOFR; LME metals are USD-denominated.
  • Convenience yield: calibrated from cash-3M spread; negative in contango (surplus), zero/positive in backwardation (deficit). Use LME published lease rate as starting input.
  • Long-run marginal cost: copper ~6,500–8,000 USD/t; aluminium ~1,800–2,500 USD/t; zinc ~2,000–2,800 USD/t. Derived from published cost-curve data; review annually.
  • Stock-convenience yield relationship: calibrate the sensitivity of convenience yield to LME stock levels; this relationship must be re-estimated when warehouse rules or reporting change.
Validation methods
  • Cost-of-carry upper bound: F(T) ≤ S · e^((r+s)T); if breached, a documented LME storage arbitrage exists — buy spot, deliver to LME, sell forward.
  • Lease rate consistency: convenience yield implied by the curve should track published LME lease rate quotes within bid-ask; persistent divergence signals input data or model error.
  • Stock correlation: implied convenience yield should be negatively correlated with LME registered stock levels; a decorrelation event must be investigated and documented.
  • DV01 monotonic decay: sensitivity per pillar should decrease with maturity for a cost-of-carry curve; flat or rising DV01 at long end flags over-reliance on parametric extrapolation.
  • Back-test vs LME official settlements: rolling comparison per maturity bucket (cash, 3M, 15M, 27M); RMSE by bucket tracked across rolling 6-month window.
ⓘ Key risk
The prompt-date trap

The LME daily prompt-date convention is unique among major commodity markets. A curve built on monthly pillars will produce systematically wrong prices for the daily forward strip — and most hedges against near-term delivery are sized against the daily prompt dates. Verify that your bootstrap correctly handles the LME daily convention before the first live trade.
